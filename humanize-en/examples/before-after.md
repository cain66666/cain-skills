## Example 1: LinkedIn post (standard humanization)

### Raw draft

```
Your peers validate your posts.

Your clients don't.

I see this pattern constantly. A coach gets 80 likes from other coaches. Zero DMs from actual buyers.

Then they assume LinkedIn isn't working.

It's working. Just for the wrong audience.

Here's what's happening:

When you write for people who do what you do, you naturally use insider language. You reference frameworks they recognize. You signal competence in ways that impress your field.

Other coaches nod along.

But your ideal client, the overwhelmed founder, the exhausted executive, the person who actually needs you, scrolls right past.

Not because you're not good enough.
Because nothing in your content made them feel seen.

There's a difference between content that earns respect from peers and content that creates urgency in buyers.

One gets you credibility in the room.
The other gets you inbound.

You can have both. But only if you know which one you're writing for before you hit publish.

The fix isn't more posts. It's a positioning question you answer before every single one:

"Who is this actually for, and what do they need to feel to take the next step?"

Peers need to be impressed.
Buyers need to feel understood.

Most LinkedIn content is optimized for the first group, wondering why the second group never shows up.

Have you ever caught yourself writing for your peers without realizing it?
```

### Diagnosis

- **Hook strength: 8/10** — actually strong. Direct, contradictory. Keep.
- **Factual texture: 3/10** — only one specific (80 likes). Rest is abstract.
- **Emotional register: 4/10** — observational but flat. No clear feeling beyond "noticing a pattern."
- **Rhythm: 5/10** — has some short fragments but generally uniform.
- **AI-likeness inverse: 4/10** — heavy with parallel structures, triplets, classic AI patterns.
- **Closing: 3/10** — "Have you ever caught yourself..." is the canonical AI close.

**Top issues:**
1. Three parallel constructions in a row ("X. Y." / "Not X. Y." / "One X. Other Y.")
2. Generic ICP painting ("overwhelmed founder, exhausted executive")
3. Generic CTA close

### Humanized version

```
Your peers validate your posts.
Your clients don't.

I see this every week. A coach gets 80+ likes on her best post all quarter. Every one from another coach. Zero DMs from anyone who'd ever swipe a card for her.

She tells me LinkedIn is broken.

It isn't. She's just writing for the wrong room.

When you write about what you do, you reach for the language of your field. Frameworks. Acronyms. The little signals that say "I know my craft." Your peers see those signals instantly and tap like.

But the founder drowning in her inbox at 11pm. The exec who hasn't slept properly in three weeks. They scroll right past. The content isn't bad. It's just that nothing in it made them feel seen.

Writing that earns respect from your peers and writing that gets a stranger to message you on a Tuesday morning are two completely different skills. Same words, often. Totally different effect.

Peers want to be impressed.
Buyers want to feel less alone at midnight.

Most coaches I know are quietly winning peer approval, then privately wondering why nobody's buying.

The fix isn't posting more. It's one question, asked before you hit publish:

Who is this actually for, and what do they need to feel before they DM me?

That's the whole game.
```

### What changed

- **Specific scene anchor:** "every week" + "best post all quarter" + "swipe a card" (instead of "constantly" + "actual buyers"). One concrete added, two existing ones sharpened.
- **Sensory ICP detail:** "founder drowning in her inbox at 11pm" + "exec who hasn't slept properly in three weeks" (instead of "overwhelmed founder, exhausted executive"). Time + sensation, not categorical labels.
- **Broke parallel negation:** "Not because you're not good enough. Because nothing in your content..." → "The content isn't bad. It's just that nothing in it made them feel seen." Same meaning, no AI pattern.
- **Asymmetric parallelism:** "Peers want to be impressed. Buyers want to feel less alone at midnight." Original was symmetric ("Peers need X. Buyers need Y"); humanized breaks symmetry by making one concrete.
- **Closer surgery:** "Have you ever caught yourself..." → "That's the whole game." Abrupt punch instead of generic CTA.
- **Tonal sharpening:** "Most coaches I see are quietly optimizing for peer respect, then wondering why nobody buys" → "quietly winning peer approval, then privately wondering why nobody's buying." Same observation, slightly more wry.

### What was NOT changed

- Hook (already strong)
- Core message and structure
- The positioning question (the actual takeaway)
- "Writing that earns respect... different skills" sentence — already nuanced enough

## Example 2: LinkedIn post (premium with fabricated story)

Same raw draft as Example 1. Premium version uses an explicit fabricated anecdote (only do this when user requests "demo" or "premium with invented story" — never publish as real).

### Premium version

```
Maya messaged me at 11pm last March. Screenshot of 147 likes on her latest post. Caption: "LinkedIn is officially broken for coaches."

She was wrong about which part was broken.

The post was about boundary-setting for female founders. Solid frameworks. Three actionable tips. The comments section read like a coach support group. Forty-some likes from other coaches saying "yes!" and "this!" and "preach!"

Zero DMs from a founder. Zero calls booked. Zero people who would ever pay her.

We got on a call the next morning. I asked her one question: "When you wrote that post, were you imagining a coach who'd respect you, or a founder lying awake at 2am sure she's a fraud?"

She didn't answer for what felt like a full minute.

Then: "Oh god. I was writing for the LinkedIn comments section."

She didn't post for a week. When she came back, she wrote about Sarah, a former client who hadn't taken a real day off in four years and broke down crying in their third session because she couldn't remember her sister's birthday. No framework. No "5 ways to..." Just one specific woman who couldn't remember she had a sister.

Three days later: eleven inbound DMs. Two of them booked paid sessions that week. One said, "I read this in the bathroom at work and just sat there for fifteen minutes."

Maya's old post: 147 likes, 0 DMs. Validation from people who do what she does.
Maya's new post: 23 likes, 11 DMs. Recognition from people who need what she offers.

Same person. Same writing skill. Different room.

Writing that impresses your peers and writing that gets a stranger to message you on a Tuesday morning are two completely different skills. Same words sometimes. Totally different effect.

Peers want to be impressed.
Buyers want to feel less alone at midnight.

Most coaches are quietly winning the wrong game. Every "great post!" from another coach feels like proof things are working. It's not proof. It's the algorithm matching you with the wrong room.

The fix isn't more posts. It's one question, asked before you hit publish:

Who is this actually for, and what do they need to feel before they DM me?

That's the whole game.
```

### What makes this premium

- **In medias res hook:** drops reader directly into a specific moment (named person + time + action).
- **Story-within-a-story:** Maya's new post is ABOUT Sarah. This shows what "writing for buyers" looks like, instead of explaining it.
- **Specificity escalation:** "Sarah... couldn't remember her sister's birthday" → "couldn't remember she had a sister." Same fact, escalated meaning.
- **Reader-quote from a stranger:** "I read this in the bathroom at work and just sat there for fifteen minutes." Concrete proof of impact.
- **Numerical climax:** "147/0 vs 23/11" lets reader see the mechanism in one glance.

**Critical disclaimer:** Maya, Sarah, and all specifics in this version are fabricated. If publishing, every detail must either be real or clearly marked as illustrative. Never present invented specifics as real claims.

## Example 3: Cold email

### Raw draft

```
Subject: Quick question

Hi John,

I hope this email finds you well. I came across your profile on LinkedIn and was impressed with the work you're doing at Acme Corp. I wanted to reach out because I think we might be able to help you.

Our platform leverages AI to streamline your customer support workflows and unlock unprecedented efficiency gains. Many of our clients have seen significant improvements in their key metrics after implementing our solution.

Would you be open to a quick 15-minute call next week to explore if there's a fit? I have availability Tuesday at 2pm or Thursday at 10am.

Looking forward to hearing from you.

Best regards,
Sarah
```

### Diagnosis

- **Subject line: 2/10** — generic "Quick question" is overused.
- **Opening: 1/10** — "I hope this email finds you well" + "I came across your profile" = canonical cold email tells.
- **Specificity: 1/10** — zero concrete reasons WHY this person, zero proof points.
- **Voice: corporate brochure, no human warmth.

### Humanized version

```
Subject: Your post about renewals on Tuesday

John,

Saw your post about expansion revenue eating up CS bandwidth — specifically the bit about your team running renewal reviews on calls instead of in CRM data.

We solved a near-identical problem for a Series B SaaS last quarter. They went from 4-hour renewal prep per account to ~25 minutes, and caught $340k of churn risk that was hiding in product usage data.

Worth 20 minutes to see if the same approach maps to Acme? If not — happy to send the playbook regardless.

Sarah

P.S. Saw you're hiring a CS lead. The candidate we placed at [redacted] last month might be a fit. Happy to make the intro either way.
```

### What changed

- **Subject:** "Quick question" → "Your post about renewals on Tuesday." Specific reference proves you actually read something.
- **Opening:** Removed "hope this finds you well" entirely. Replaced with one specific observation from John's recent activity.
- **Value claim with numbers:** Instead of "significant improvements", "4-hour to ~25 minutes" and "$340k of churn risk." Specific and noisy.
- **Soft ask:** Replaced "open to a quick 15-minute call" + scheduling friction with "Worth 20 minutes to see if it maps?" Lower commitment, less salesy.
- **Trust signal in close:** "Happy to send the playbook regardless" — proves you're not just chasing the meeting.
- **P.S. that adds value beyond the pitch.** Optional, but shows you're paying attention.

### What was preserved

- Brief length (cold email norms)
- Single clear ask
- First-name basis
- Sender's name only

## Example 4: Email sequence — opening of email 2 of 5

### Raw draft (email 2, nurture/value)

```
Hi John,

Following up on my previous email. I wanted to share some additional insights that might be helpful as you think about your customer support strategy.

Many of our clients struggle with the same challenges you're facing. They often feel overwhelmed by the sheer volume of tickets, frustrated by long response times, and worried about the impact on customer satisfaction.

The good news is that there's a proven framework that can help. By implementing the right combination of automation, training, and process optimization, you can transform your support operations and deliver an exceptional customer experience.

I've attached a guide that walks through this framework in detail. I hope you find it valuable.

Looking forward to your thoughts.

Best,
Sarah
```

### Diagnosis

This is the canonical "email 2" trap: it pretends to provide value but actually just teases another email. The "framework" is undefined. The "guide" is the actual content; the email is filler.

Also: "Following up on my previous email" — empty opener. "I hope you find it valuable" — empty closer. Three triplets of struggles ("overwhelmed... frustrated... worried"). One "transformative" claim without evidence.

### Humanized version

```
Subject: The renewal review thing — here's the playbook

John,

Mentioned a 4-hour-to-25-minute fix for renewal prep in my last email. Promised the playbook.

Two pages. Not a download form. Just the actual thing:

[link]

Specifically — pages 1-2 cover the data structure switch that does most of the work. Page 3 is the part most teams skip (the review cadence change).

If this is useful, you don't need to reply. If it's not — I'd rather know so I can stop emailing about renewals.

Sarah
```

### What changed

- **Subject specific:** references the actual thing being sent, not generic "following up."
- **Cut filler opening:** "Following up on my previous email" → directly references the specific value from email 1.
- **Removed generic "challenges":** no triplet of woes. The reader knows their own problems; you don't need to summarize them.
- **Actual content delivery:** no "framework" gate, just "two pages, not a download form, the actual thing." Builds trust.
- **Pointer to most useful part:** "pages 1-2 cover X. Page 3 is the part most teams skip" — shows you actually read your own playbook.
- **No-pressure close:** "If this is useful, you don't need to reply. If it's not — I'd rather know." Inverts typical pressure pattern.

## Example 5: Sales copy — hero + value prop

### Raw draft

```
[HERO]
Transform Your Customer Support with AI

Our cutting-edge platform leverages artificial intelligence to streamline your support operations and unlock unprecedented productivity gains.

[VALUE PROP 1]
Lightning-Fast Resolution

Reduce ticket resolution times by up to 80% with our intelligent automation engine that handles routine queries seamlessly.

[VALUE PROP 2]
Effortless Scaling

Scale your support operations effortlessly without adding headcount. Our platform grows with you, handling 10x volume with the same team.

[VALUE PROP 3]
Customer-First Experience

Deliver exceptional customer experiences with personalized, context-aware responses that delight your users.
```

### Diagnosis

Hero is generic. "Transform with AI" describes 10,000 SaaS products. Value props are all telling, no showing. "Up to 80%", "10x volume", "delight" — adjective-heavy, proof-light. "Cutting-edge", "lightning-fast", "effortless", "seamlessly" — six forbidden words in 60 words of copy.

### Humanized version

```
[HERO]
Your team is closing 30% of tickets on autopilot. 
They just don't know it yet.

Most "AI for support" platforms add a chatbot. We remove the tickets 
that shouldn't have been tickets.

[VALUE PROP 1]
We caught 14,000 duplicate tickets last quarter at SimilarCo

Their team had been answering the same 47 questions for two years 
without realizing it. Now those tickets close themselves before they 
hit an agent's queue. Cost saved: about $84k/year, three FTE-worth.

[VALUE PROP 2]
You won't 10x volume. You'll cap headcount at what it is now.

Support teams don't grow 10x — that's a vendor fantasy. What 
actually happens is volume grows 2-3x while your team stays at six 
people. We get you the math to make that survivable.

[VALUE PROP 3]
The ticket that gets a personal reply gets it from the right agent

Not faster. Not "AI-powered." Routed to the agent with the closest 
match in resolution history. Customer feels seen because someone who 
actually understands the problem is writing back — within the same 
hour they'd be waiting for an autoresponder anyway.
```

### What changed

- **Hero specific paradox:** "Your team is closing 30% on autopilot. They just don't know it yet." Specific number + counter-intuitive claim.
- **Anti-position to category:** "We remove the tickets that shouldn't have been tickets" — differentiates against the obvious competitor pattern.
- **Value prop 1 has a named customer + specific number:** SimilarCo, 14,000 tickets, 47 questions, $84k savings, three FTE. Every claim has a noisy number.
- **Value prop 2 is anti-claim:** "You won't 10x volume" — explicitly rejects the vendor fantasy the original asserted. Builds trust by being honest about limits.
- **Value prop 3 is operational truth:** instead of "delightful experiences," describes the actual mechanism that produces them.

## Example 6: Newsletter opening

### Raw draft

```
Hi everyone,

I hope you had a wonderful week! As we navigate the ever-evolving landscape of B2B sales, I wanted to share some thoughts on a topic that's been on my mind lately: the importance of authentic connection in a digital-first world.

In today's fast-paced business environment, it's easy to lose sight of what really matters. We get caught up in optimizing funnels, A/B testing subject lines, and chasing the latest tactics. But at the end of the day, sales is still about people connecting with people.

This week, I want to explore three key principles that can help you cultivate more meaningful relationships with your prospects...
```

### Diagnosis

This is canonical AI newsletter opening. "Wonderful week" + "ever-evolving landscape" + "today's fast-paced" + "at the end of the day" — four AI tells in three sentences. Topic ("authentic connection") is generic. No anchor in reality.

### Humanized version

```
Last Wednesday I watched a sales rep send the same templated email to 47 prospects in 22 minutes. I asked him why. He said: "Manager wants 300 a day, I have to."

I thought about that all weekend.

The question isn't whether outbound is dead. (It isn't.) The question is whether you're sending 300 emails or sending 12 — and if 300, what you're optimizing for.

This week I want to walk through what changed for one team that cut their volume by 90% and doubled meetings booked. Not because they "found the magic message." Because they stopped writing for the activity dashboard and started writing for the person opening the email.
```

### What changed

- **Specific opener:** "Last Wednesday I watched a sales rep send the same templated email to 47 prospects in 22 minutes" — anchored scene with specific number and time.
- **Direct quote:** "Manager wants 300 a day, I have to" — implies real overheard conversation.
- **Personal aside:** "I thought about that all weekend" — small admission of emotional reaction.
- **Acknowledged objection:** "The question isn't whether outbound is dead. (It isn't.)" — parenthetical handles the predictable counterargument.
- **Specific story preview:** "one team that cut volume by 90% and doubled meetings" — not vague "I'll share three principles."

## Example 7: Blog post opening

### Raw draft

```
The Future of Customer Success: How AI is Transforming the Industry

In today's digital age, customer success has emerged as one of the most critical functions in modern SaaS businesses. As companies increasingly recognize the importance of customer retention and expansion, the role of CS teams has evolved dramatically over the past decade.

Now, with the rise of artificial intelligence, we're witnessing yet another transformation. AI-powered tools are reshaping how CS teams operate, enabling them to deliver more personalized, proactive, and impactful experiences at scale.

In this article, we'll explore the key trends driving this transformation, examine real-world examples of AI in action, and provide actionable insights you can apply to your own CS organization.
```

### Diagnosis

Three AI-tell openings stacked: "In today's digital age" + "emerged as one of the most critical" + "yet another transformation." Setup paragraph telling reader what they'll read (classic AI structure). Generic claim about AI without evidence.

### Humanized version

```
A CSM at a Series B SaaS told me last month that she has 87 accounts. She used to have 12.

Her churn rate is the same as it was three years ago.

Either she's three times better at her job, or her job changed in some way that made volume not matter as much. The answer matters because every CS leader I've talked to in the last year is asking the same question: how big is the leverage from AI, really, and where does it break?

I spent the last six weeks looking at this with three teams that range from 20 accounts per CSM to 200. Here's what's actually working, what isn't, and where the unsexy parts of the job — the ones nobody talks about — quietly became the bottleneck.
```

### What changed

- **Specific human in the opening:** A CSM with 87 accounts (up from 12). One data point, one specific person.
- **Counter-intuitive claim:** churn rate unchanged. Forces the reader to wonder why.
- **Honest framing of the question:** "how big is the leverage from AI, really, and where does it break?" — anti-hype.
- **Specific research basis:** "three teams that range from 20 to 200 accounts per CSM." Tells reader the analysis isn't just opinion.
- **Promised content:** "what's working, what isn't, and where the unsexy parts became the bottleneck." Specific, not generic.

## Cross-cutting patterns

Reviewing all seven examples, the same moves appear:

1. **Replace categorical descriptions with one specific instance.** "Coaches" → "Maya." "Customer support teams" → "SimilarCo, 14,000 duplicate tickets." "CSMs" → "A CSM with 87 accounts."

2. **Replace adjective claims with number claims.** "Significant improvement" → "4-hour to 25-minute." "Lightning-fast" → "47 questions, $84k saved."

3. **Replace setup paragraphs with action.** "In this article we'll explore..." → "A CSM told me last month..."

4. **Replace generic CTAs with specific asks or no asks.** "Looking forward to your thoughts" → "If this is useful, you don't need to reply. If not — I'd rather know."

5. **Acknowledge objections you have evidence for.** "You won't 10x volume" instead of pretending 10x is realistic.

6. **End on substance, not summary.** Every humanized version ends on the strongest substantive sentence, not a recap.

The moves are simple. Applying them consistently is what separates humanized output from AI-flavored output.

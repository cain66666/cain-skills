# Content Types — Specific Rules

Different content types need different humanization approaches. The three layers (factual texture → emotional register → rhythm) apply universally, but the **target voice, structure, and constraints** vary.

Read the section matching your input type before rewriting.

## 1. LinkedIn / professional social media posts

### Target reading time
~15-45 seconds. Quick scan, then "See more" expansion, then ~30-second read.

### Length sweet spot
- Punchy take: 600-900 chars
- Standard post: 1100-1500 chars
- Personal story: 1500-2100 chars (longer is OK if narrative is strong)
- Hard cap: 2200 chars (LinkedIn truncation issues beyond)

### Structure
```
Line 1: HOOK (5-12 words, must work without context)
Line 2: blank
Line 3-5: Setup / open loop (1-2 short sentences expanding the hook)
Line 6: blank
Line 7-15: Body (story / argument / evidence)
Line 16: blank
Line 17: CLOSE (question OR punch line OR abrupt stop)
```

Line breaks are aggressive on LinkedIn. Almost every sentence on its own line. White space drives engagement.

### Hook patterns that work
- **Scene anchor**: "Tuesday, 9pm, third coffee. My co-founder calls."
- **Confession**: "I almost fired the only person who understood our product."
- **Contradiction**: "Everyone says hire slow. I just hired in 48 hours."
- **Shock number**: "In 6 months I deleted $40k of marketing spend. Revenue went up."
- **Micro-cliffhanger**: "Last Tuesday I got an email I'd been waiting four years to see."
- **Direct callout**: "If you're a SaaS founder under $1M ARR, this is for you. And it might piss you off."

### Hooks that DON'T work (avoid)
- "The power of..."
- "Why you should..."
- "Here are X things..."
- "Have you ever wondered..."
- "Let me tell you about..."
- "Today I want to talk about..."
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "[Topic] is one of the most important things..."

### Close patterns that work
- **Punch line**: short sentence that crystallizes the post
- **Experience question**: "Have you ever fired someone who was technically doing fine?"
- **Abrupt stop**: end on the strongest substantive sentence

### Closes that DON'T work
- "Thoughts?"
- "Agree?"
- "What do you think?"
- "Let me know in the comments."
- "I hope this resonates."
- Any recap paragraph

### Voice for LinkedIn
- First person
- Slightly contrarian or observational
- Confident without arrogance
- Dry humor OK
- Vulnerability OK (passionate authenticity + transparent authenticity per Audrezet 2018)
- Anti-corporate (no "leverage", "synergy", "stakeholders")

### Critical: line breaks vs paragraphs
LinkedIn line breaks ≠ traditional paragraphs. One thought = one line break, even mid-paragraph. This creates the visual rhythm that drives dwell time. AI defaults to traditional paragraph structure — fix this.

Bad (AI default):
> I learned an important lesson last week. A client called me at 9pm crying because she had just made a major mistake. We talked through it together and figured out a path forward. The key takeaway was that vulnerability is a strength.

Good (LinkedIn native):
> A client called me at 9pm last Tuesday. Crying.
>
> She'd just sent a $40k proposal to the wrong person.
>
> We didn't fix it that night. We just talked.
>
> Sometimes the work isn't strategy. It's holding the phone.

## 2. Single email (cold, nurture, transactional)

Email is the most relationship-aware text type. The same content lands differently depending on whether the recipient knows you.

### Subject line is a hook
The hook in email is the subject line. Apply same rules as LinkedIn hooks: specific, curiosity-creating, no generic openers.

### Cold email
- Open with one specific reason you're reaching out (NOT "I came across your profile"). Reference a specific post, decision, hire, launch — something only someone who actually read about them would know.
- ONE clear ask. Not three.
- ~80-150 words total.
- Sign off short. No "Best regards, with utmost respect and looking forward to your prompt response."

### Avoid in cold email
- "I hope this email finds you well"
- "I trust this message finds you doing great"
- "I'm reaching out because..."
- "I came across..."
- "Quick question:" (overused)
- "Just circling back"

### Nurture email
- Story-driven, no pitching
- Personal voice
- Short or long both work, depending on relationship depth
- End on something interesting, not a CTA

### Transactional email
- Direct, brief
- One purpose per email
- Friendly but not chatty
- Confirm action, set expectation, close

### Voice for email
- Warmer than LinkedIn
- More direct than sales copy
- Conversational
- Use the recipient's name once, in the body, not the opener
- Sign off with first name only (last name only in formal B2B)

## 3. Email sequences (multi-message campaigns)

Email sequences need consistent voice across messages but DIFFERENT jobs per email. Don't humanize each email in isolation — humanize the SEQUENCE.

### Typical sequence structure
- **Email 1 (intro)** — Why I'm in their inbox. One specific reason. No pitch.
- **Email 2 (value)** — Insight or resource. Demonstrates you understand their world. No pitch.
- **Email 3 (story / proof)** — Case study or story showing the problem you solve.
- **Email 4 (pitch)** — Direct offer with proof.
- **Email 5 (objection handling)** — Address #1 concern.
- **Email 6 (final)** — Last chance, no apology.

### Voice consistency rules
- Same author signature feeling across all 6
- Same recurring tics if the author uses them (e.g., always starts with a one-line scene, always closes with a question)
- Same level of formality
- Same emotional register baseline

### Sequence-level humanization checks
- Does each email feel like the same person wrote it?
- Do the emails reference each other naturally? ("I sent you an article on Tuesday — did it land?")
- Is the relationship deepening? Email 5 should sound more familiar than Email 1.
- Are you escalating in directness, not aggression?

### Pitch email specifically
- Lead with a specific result you've delivered for someone like them
- Make the offer crystal clear in one sentence
- Acknowledge one objection (transparent authenticity)
- Soft CTA — "Worth a 20-min call to see if it fits?" beats "BOOK NOW"

## 4. Sales copy / landing page text

Different from social media: reader is in **buying mode**, not entertainment mode. The job is conversion, not engagement.

### Length norms
- Hero section: 1-2 sentences
- Value props: 50-100 words each, 3-5 of them
- Long-form sales page: 2000-5000 words (in sections)

### Section-by-section logic
Most landing pages need:
1. **Hero** — One specific result for one specific person, in 1-2 sentences
2. **Pain section** — Show them you understand the specific pain. Use their words.
3. **Solution intro** — Brief preview of what you do
4. **Mechanism** — How it works (3-5 steps or pillars)
5. **Proof** — Specific results from specific customers
6. **Objection handling** — Address top 3 concerns directly
7. **Offer** — What they get, what it costs, guarantee
8. **Close** — One last reason to act

### Humanization for sales copy
The trap: sales copy feels "salesy" or "AI-written" when it talks AT the customer instead of describing them.

**AI-coded sales copy:**
> "Our platform leverages cutting-edge AI to streamline your workflow and unlock unprecedented productivity gains."

**Humanized version:**
> "You probably spend 2 hours every Monday morning building the same reports you built last Monday. This kills that."

The shift: from generic capability claims to specific customer experience.

### Concrete proof beats adjectives
- "Customers love it" — no
- "Sarah saved 8 hours/week. Tom's team closed 3x more deals in Q2." — yes

### Voice for sales copy
- Confident (not pushy)
- Specific (not generic)
- Buyer-aware (uses their language, not yours)
- Honest about limits (transparent authenticity converts better than maximalist claims)

## 5. Blog posts / long-form articles

Longer rhythm allowed. Multiple ideas. Structure matters.

### Length norms
- Short post: 500-800 words
- Standard: 1200-2000 words
- Deep dive: 3000+ words

### Structure
- Strong hook in opening paragraph (same hook patterns as LinkedIn but slightly longer)
- Section headers (H2) for navigation — but USE THEM SPARINGLY. 5-7 headers for a 2000-word piece, not 15.
- One idea per section
- Concrete examples in every section
- Natural conclusion (not a recap)

### Voice for blog
- More authoritative than LinkedIn
- Depth allowed
- Personal experience welcome
- Opinion welcome (this isn't journalism unless it is)

### Humanization specific to blog
- The "AI blog post" tell is **uniform paragraph length, uniform sentence rhythm, uniform tone**. Fix by varying:
  - Paragraph length (some 1-line, some 3-line, some 7-line)
  - Including a personal aside in one section
  - Letting one section be shorter and more punchy than the others
  - Breaking the pattern with a fragment or one-line paragraph occasionally

### Things blog AI loves and humans don't
- "In this article, we'll explore..." — DELETE
- "Before we dive in, let's first..." — DELETE
- "Stay tuned for next week's post..." — usually unnecessary
- Numbered lists of 5 or 7 (always 5 or 7!) — vary the number
- Recap paragraph at the end — DELETE

## 6. Newsletters

Between social and blog. Personal voice in long-form. Casual but coherent.

### Structure
- Often opens with a personal anecdote or observation
- Main content (could be one idea or several short ones)
- Optional links/resources section
- Sign-off

### Voice for newsletter
- Most personal of all formats
- First-name basis with reader (implicit or explicit)
- Reference shared history with the audience if it exists
- Loose structure is fine — feels more like a letter than an article

### Humanization for newsletter
- The newsletter's "human-ness" comes from the **opening personal anecdote**. If that anecdote is generic, the whole newsletter falls flat. Treat the opener with extra care — must be specific, sensory, scene-based.
- Newsletters can violate "blog rules" — fragments, parentheticals, asides are MORE welcome here than in blog.
- The sign-off is part of the voice. "P.S." sections are an opportunity for warmth or one extra concrete detail.

## 7. Twitter / X posts

Very different rules. Short, punchy, often standalone.

### Length norms
- Single tweet: 280 chars
- Thread: 5-15 tweets

### Single tweet humanization
- Every word matters. No filler.
- Specifics > abstractions, but you have less room for them.
- Voice carries even more weight than in longer formats.

### Thread humanization
- First tweet IS the hook. Same rules as LinkedIn hooks.
- Each subsequent tweet should be a complete thought (not "and then..." continuations).
- Avoid the AI thread template: "1/ Today we'll explore X. 2/ First, let's understand..."
- Real threads have rhythm — some short, some longer, some single-line punchlines.

## 8. Other prose

For any prose-based content not covered above, apply the universal three layers (factual texture, emotional register, rhythm) and these defaults:

- Read the input twice. Identify what kind of text it WANTS to be.
- Match voice to context (formal vs casual, public vs private, professional vs personal).
- Cut everything that doesn't earn its place.
- End on substance, not summary.

## Cross-cutting principle

Whatever the content type: **the strongest version of any text is the one only one specific human could have written**. Length, structure, and voice vary by type. The pursuit of unfakeable specificity does not.

## 1. Factual texture techniques

### Specific numbers with noise

Replace round numbers with realistic-feeling specifics.

**Bad:** "Got 100 likes on the post"
**Good:** "Got 127 likes on that post"
**Better:** "Got 127 likes by Tuesday morning. Zero of them mattered."

The reason "127" works better than "100": humans don't measure things in round numbers. "100" feels like a placeholder; "127" feels like an actual count someone looked at.

Same with money: "$2,300" beats "$2k", "around $47k" beats "around $50k".

### Named entities

Generic categories die. Names live.

**Bad:** "A coach I work with"
**Better:** "A coach I work with, Maya"
**Better still:** "Maya runs a coaching practice for early-stage women founders in NYC"

The progression: category → name → name + context. Don't always go all the way; the right depth depends on length and relevance. But always at least one step beyond generic.

When real names aren't available and you can't fabricate, you can use:
- Role + identifying detail: "the third hire we made in 2023"
- Implied identification: "a client whose company you've probably heard of"
- Composite acknowledged: "this is a composite of three clients, all named 'Sarah'"

### Time anchors

A specific time roots an abstract claim in reality.

**Bad:** "I learned something important recently"
**Better:** "Last Tuesday I learned something important"
**Better still:** "Last Tuesday at 6:14am, sitting in a coworking on Folsom, I figured out what I'd been missing for two years"

The hierarchy: vague → specific day → specific moment with location.

Time anchors signal: this was real. Real events have when-where coordinates. Generic claims don't.

### Sensory details

The fastest path to "this person was actually there" is one sensory detail.

- Visual: "her laptop battery icon was red"
- Auditory: "the espresso machine kept screaming"
- Tactile: "the conference room chair smelled like someone else's lunch"
- Physical sensation: "I felt my jaw clench"
- Specific objects: "she had three open Notion tabs and a half-eaten croissant"

These don't need to be central to the point. They're texture. One per piece is often enough.

### Direct quotes

A remembered (or claimed-to-be-remembered) quote is enormously human-sounding.

**Bad:** "She told me she was overwhelmed"
**Better:** "She said, 'I haven't slept properly in three weeks.'"

Even paraphrased quotes work: "She said something like 'I can't keep doing this' and I knew it was time."

Quotes work because they imply specific memory of specific words. Generic claims don't carry this implication.

### Anti-fabrication rule

The single most important rule: **never fabricate specific people, dates, events, or numbers unless explicitly asked.**

This is hard because specifics are exactly what makes text feel human. The tension is real.

Resolution:
- If you have author input (real specifics from the writer): use them.
- If you don't and the text is generic: flag this. Ask for input. Don't invent.
- If the user explicitly asks for "demo version" or "fabricated story" or "premium version with invented anecdote": create plausible specifics, mark clearly as fabricated.
- If you can use composite or atmospheric details that aren't factual claims about real people (e.g., "11pm on a Tuesday" applied to a representative customer): okay, but use sparingly.

The reader trusts text. Fabricated specifics that get exposed destroy that trust permanently. When in doubt, abstract is safer than fabricated.

## 2. Emotional register techniques

### One feeling, committed

Pick ONE dominant feeling and let it run the piece. Don't try to be funny, vulnerable, contrarian, and inspiring in the same 1500 chars.

The most common LinkedIn-friendly registers:
- **Quiet contempt** — for an industry pattern, a common mistake, a "thought leader" cliché
- **Confession** — for a mistake or wrong belief the author held
- **Tired conviction** — for saying something for the hundredth time
- **Dry amusement** — for absurdities the author has witnessed
- **Awe** — for genuinely surprising observations
- **Frustration** — for problems the author is wrestling with
- **Pride (guarded)** — for accomplishments without bragging

Once you've picked the register, run a check: does every paragraph carry this feeling? If a paragraph is tonally neutral, either match it to the register or cut it.

### Show, don't label

This is the most common rewrite move.

**Bad (labels):**
- "I was frustrated"
- "It was disappointing"
- "She felt overwhelmed"
- "We were excited"

**Good (shows):**
- "I closed the laptop. Walked outside. Stood in the parking lot for ten minutes."
- "She replied two days later, with three words: 'I can't anymore.'"
- "I read the email twice. Then I called my co-founder."

The pattern: write the moment that produces the feeling in the reader's body. Don't tell them what to feel.

### Emotional asymmetry

AI loves balance. Humans don't.

If you're contrasting two things, make one part specific and the other generic. This asymmetry signals authenticity.

**Symmetric (AI feel):**
"Peers want to be impressed. Buyers want to feel understood."

**Asymmetric (human feel):**
"Peers want to be impressed. Buyers want to feel less alone at midnight."

The first sounds like a slogan. The second sounds like someone who's been thinking about a specific buyer for a long time.

### Permission to be unresolved

AI wraps everything. Human writing can end on tension.

A LinkedIn post can end with the question still hanging. A blog can close on the problem unsolved. Don't force resolution. Sometimes "I'm still figuring this out" is more powerful than any conclusion.

This is risky for AI to do because it was trained to always conclude. Lean into the discomfort. Unresolved endings are highly human.

## 3. Rhythm engineering techniques

### Sentence length variance

Target distribution for a 1500-char post:
- At least 1 sentence ≤ 5 words (fragment)
- At least 1 sentence ≥ 22 words
- Average sentence length: 10-15 words
- Standard deviation: > 7

Run a mental check after rewriting: scan sentence lengths. If they cluster in 10-15 word range with little variance, you have AI rhythm.

### Fragments

Fragments aren't grammar errors — they're rhythm tools.

Use them for:
- **Emphasis:** "And then it broke. Just like that."
- **Scene painting:** "Tuesday. 9pm. Third coffee."
- **Punch:** "Big mistake. Took me a year to fix."
- **Single-word punctuation:** "Honestly?", "Look.", "Anyway."

Don't overuse. Two-to-three fragments in a 1500-char post is enough. More starts to feel like AI mimicking fragmented style.

### Parenthetical asides

A parenthetical aside is a tiny acknowledgment of the human voice. It signals: I'm thinking out loud, this isn't a press release.

Examples:
- "I tried it twice (don't ask) and gave up."
- "She replied within an hour (which is unusual for her)."
- "It worked, eventually (after three terrible weeks)."

One per piece is usually enough. Place it where a natural aside would fit, not just anywhere.

### Conjunction starters

Starting sentences with "And", "But", "So" — banned by English teachers, embraced by good writers. Highly human-sounding.

Use them when:
- You want continuity with the previous sentence: "And then she called me again."
- You want a sharp pivot: "But that's not what happened."
- You want consequence: "So I quit."

Don't start every sentence this way. Sprinkle.

### Variable opener types

AI often starts consecutive sentences the same way ("I did X. I saw Y. I learned Z.").

Vary openers:
- Pronoun: "I did X."
- Conjunction: "But Y happened."
- Question word: "What no one tells you is..."
- Time anchor: "Last Tuesday, ..."
- Scene: "The room was empty."
- Dialogue: "'You're wrong,' she said."

In a 10-sentence piece, no more than 3 should start the same way.

## 4. Hook construction

The hook is the first 1-2 sentences (or subject line for email). It determines whether anyone reads the rest.

### Hook patterns (proven)

**Scene anchor**
"Tuesday, 9pm, third coffee. My co-founder calls."
Anatomy: specific time + specific setting + specific action that implies tension.

**Confession**
"I almost fired the only person who understood our product."
Anatomy: first-person admission of something risky or counter-intuitive.

**Contradiction**
"Everyone says hire slow. I just hired in 48 hours."
Anatomy: state the consensus, then break it.

**Shock number**
"In 6 months I deleted $40k of marketing spend. Revenue went up."
Anatomy: specific number + counter-intuitive result.

**Micro-cliffhanger**
"Last Tuesday I got an email I'd been waiting four years to see."
Anatomy: specific time + reference to something undisclosed but emotionally charged.

**Counterfactual / before-after**
"Three years ago I wrote three posts a day. Now I write one a week and have 10x the reach."
Anatomy: specific past behavior + specific present result, both quantified.

**Direct callout**
"If you're a SaaS founder under $1M ARR, this is for you. It might piss you off."
Anatomy: niche identification + warning of discomfort.

### Anti-patterns

Never open with:
- "The power of..."
- "Why you should..."
- "Here are X things..."
- "Have you ever wondered..."
- "Let me tell you..."
- "Today I want to talk about..."
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- Topic statement: "Leadership is hard." (unless followed immediately by specific contradiction)

### Hook checklist

Before approving a hook, verify:
- Specific (has a number, name, time, or scene)?
- Creates curiosity (doesn't fully reveal the insight)?
- Could only this person have written it (not generic)?
- Under 130 characters (for LinkedIn, fits in pre-truncation view)?

If yes to all four: ship.

## 5. Closing construction

### Three valid endings

**Punch line**
A short, crystallizing sentence. Stops the post cold.

Examples:
- "That's the whole game."
- "Hiring is just trust, paid forward."
- "Sometimes the work isn't strategy. It's holding the phone."

The punch line should crystallize the post's insight in a way the body itself didn't quite. It rewards the reader who made it to the end.

**Experience-specific question**
A question that requires the reader's actual lived experience to answer.

Examples:
- "Have you ever fired someone who was technically doing fine?"
- "When was the last time a peer's reaction told you nothing about your work?"
- "What's the post you wrote that worked least well — and who was it for?"

NOT this kind of question:
- "Thoughts?"
- "Agree?"
- "What do you think?"

The difference: a real question requires the reader to think back to a specific situation. A generic CTA doesn't.

**Abrupt stop**
End on the strongest substantive sentence. No closer needed.

This is the bravest option. Trust the body to land without underlining.

If you can't decide between abrupt stop and punch line, lean toward abrupt stop. Less is usually more.

### Anti-patterns

Never close with:
- Summary paragraph
- "Ultimately..." / "In conclusion..." / "All in all..."
- "I hope this resonates / helps."
- "Let me know your thoughts."
- "Drop a comment if you agree."
- "Stay tuned for more."
- Motivational closer ("Keep building", "Onwards")
- "P.S. Follow me for more like this."

## 6. Story structure

The strongest LinkedIn / blog / newsletter content uses story structure. Even a "thought leadership" post benefits from one concrete narrative thread.

### Four-act story structure

1. **Scene** — Specific moment, specific person, specific stakes
2. **Tension** — What was at risk, what made this hard
3. **Turning point** — The decision, realization, surprise
4. **Aftermath** — What actually happened, with specifics

The "lesson" should be implied by the aftermath, not stated as a separate fifth act. Trust the reader.

### In medias res openings

Start in the middle of the action, not with setup.

**Bad (setup first):**
"I've been working with a coach for the past two years. Recently, she came to me with a problem. She said..."

**Good (in medias res):**
"Maya messaged me at 11pm last March. Screenshot of 147 likes. Caption: 'LinkedIn is officially broken for coaches.'"

You can always backfill context later. Lead with action.

### Story-within-a-story

A powerful technique: the protagonist of your story (e.g., Maya, a coach) tells a story about THEIR client (e.g., Sarah). This nested structure demonstrates the insight through example rather than explaining it.

"Maya wrote about Sarah, a former client who hadn't taken a real day off in four years and broke down crying in their third session because she couldn't remember her sister's birthday."

The post about peers vs buyers shows what "writing for buyers" looks like — by including a fragment of Maya's new buyer-focused post (the Sarah story). Show, don't tell, at a meta level.

### Specificity escalation

In a story, each layer should escalate specificity, not generalize.

Bad escalation: "Sarah was tired. She was very tired. She was extremely exhausted."
Good escalation: "Sarah hadn't taken a real day off in four years. She broke down crying in their third session. Because she couldn't remember her sister's birthday."

Each new piece of information adds a specific concrete detail, not an adjective.

## 7. Voice consistency

When humanizing for a specific author (vs. generic), voice match becomes critical.

### Voice components to match

1. **Vocabulary** — common words this author uses (especially their "tic words")
2. **Sentence rhythm** — typical length, variance, fragment frequency
3. **Opener patterns** — how do they typically start sentences?
4. **Closer patterns** — punch lines? questions? abrupt stops?
5. **Emotional baseline** — dry? warm? edgy? earnest?
6. **Profanity / register** — do they swear? formal or casual?
7. **Self-reference frequency** — heavy "I" or balanced with "you" / "we"?

### Voice mimicking technique

If you have 3-5 example posts from the author, scan them for:
- Words/phrases they use 2+ times (signature phrases)
- How they open posts (hook patterns they prefer)
- How they close posts
- Their typical post length

Then rewrite the input to match these patterns. Don't add new patterns that aren't in their work.

### When in doubt

If voice match is unclear, default to:
- First person
- Direct, slightly contrarian
- One personal anecdote
- Punch line or experience question close

This is a safe baseline that works for most professional contexts on LinkedIn.

## 8. Research foundation

The techniques above are not arbitrary. They're grounded in:

### Stylometric research
- **Kobak et al. 2024** ("Delving into ChatGPT usage in academic writing") — measured "excess vocabulary" of LLMs in PubMed abstracts; "delve" increased 10× post-ChatGPT, along with "underscore", "showcase", "meticulous", "commendable". This is why those words are forbidden.
- **Matsui 2024** (medRxiv) — independent confirmation of LLM-specific vocabulary in medical writing.
- **Geng & Trotta 2025** ("Human-LLM Coevolution") — once "delve" became a known AI tell, its usage dropped in academic writing as humans edited it out.

### Detection research
- **EQ-Bench Slop Score** — open benchmark measuring AI-isms in model outputs. Direct guide for which words are AI-coded.
- **Pangram DAMAGE paper 2025** — audit of humanizer tools; showed that simple "rewrite to sound human" prompts work because the AI tells are concentrated in specific patterns.
- **Liang et al. 2023** (Stanford) — GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers because limited vocabulary lowers perplexity. Implication: avoid penalizing simpler vocabulary as AI; the tells are in specific words, not simplicity overall.

### Engagement research
- **Berger & Milkman 2012** ("What Makes Online Content Viral", JMR) — content with high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety, amusement) shares more. Implication: flat emotional tone kills engagement.
- **Loewenstein 1994** ("Psychology of Curiosity") — curiosity gap as driver of attention. Implication: hooks should create gap, not deliver insight.
- **Heath & Heath, Made to Stick 2007** — specific concrete stories remembered 22× better than abstract claims.

### Authenticity research
- **Audrezet et al. 2018** (JBR) — two dimensions of authenticity: passionate (visible personal investment) + transparent (visible conflicts and limits). Both build trust.
- **Pennebaker, LIWC research** — function-word frequencies (especially "I", "my") are stronger personality markers than content words.

### Parasocial research
- **Vu, Wang, Keating, Chen 2025** (Australasian Marketing Journal) — parasocial interaction is the main mediator from source credibility to social media stickiness. Implication: people follow accounts that feel personal, not corporate.

These foundations explain why the techniques work, not just that they do.

## Quick reference: technique selection by problem

| Problem | First technique to try |
|---|---|
| Too abstract | Add one specific (number, name, time, or sensory detail) |
| Flat emotion | Pick one feeling, rewrite one paragraph as scene |
| Robotic rhythm | Add one fragment and one parenthetical |
| Weak hook | Use scene anchor or contradiction pattern |
| Recap closer | Delete recap paragraph, end on previous sentence |
| Too "smooth" | Add one mistake or doubt |
| Too generic | Replace abstract ICP with one specific person + situation |
| Sounds promotional | Allow one paragraph to end on unresolved tension |
| Voice mismatch | Mimic 2-3 specific patterns from author's existing posts |

Apply the smallest fix that solves the problem. Don't rewrite when one edit suffices.
